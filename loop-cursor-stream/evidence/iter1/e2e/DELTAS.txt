🤖 A mechanical clock measures time by converting a stored source of energy into a carefully controlled series of movements. Unlike a digital clock, which counts electronic oscillations, it relies on wheels, springs or weights, and an escapement. The energy may come from a wound mainspring or a descending weight. That energy would be released almost instantly if it were not regulated. The clock’s job is therefore not simply to make gears turn. It must allow them to turn at a predictable rate. Once that rate is established, additional gears translate the movement into seconds, minutes, hours, and sometimes audible strikes.

Most mechanical clocks store energy in a mainspring, a long strip of metal coiled inside a barrel. Turning the winding key or crown tightens the spring, increasing its potential energy. In weight-driven clocks, the equivalent energy is gravitational: lifting a weight raises it, and gravity pulls it downward later. The barrel or weight is connected to the clock’s gear train. As the spring unwinds, or the weight descends, it applies torque to the wheel. This force is transmitted through successive gears, but the train is designed to reduce speed and distribute power rather than let the first wheel spin freely.

The gear train performs two tasks. First, it carries energy from the power source toward the escapement, which is the regulating mechanism. Second, it creates ratios for the hands. A wheel turning once each hour can drive the minute hand directly, while another ratio makes the hour hand complete one revolution in twelve hours. The arrangement varies, but the wheels include a center wheel, third wheel, fourth wheel, and escape wheel. Their teeth must mesh accurately. Errors in tooth shape, spacing, lubrication, or alignment can cause friction, noise, uneven motion, or loss of power before the regulating system receives it.

The escapement controls the gear train by allowing one step at a time. It consists of an escape wheel and a device that alternately locks and releases that wheel. In a pendulum clock, this device is commonly an anchor-shaped pallet assembly. Each swing of the pendulum moves one pallet away from a tooth, allowing the escape wheel to advance. The opposite pallet then catches the next tooth. This repeated lock-and-release action is called ticking. It prevents the stored energy from rushing through the gears, while also giving the pendulum an impulse to replace energy lost to air resistance and friction.

A pendulum is effective because its period is determined mainly by its length and gravity, not by the size of its swing. For a given clock, a longer pendulum swings more slowly, and a shorter one swings more quickly. The pendulum’s regular oscillation provides a reference against which the escapement operates. Clockmakers adjust the rate by changing the effective length, often with a threaded nut beneath the bob. Raising the bob shortens the pendulum and speeds the clock; lowering it lengthens the pendulum and slows the clock. Temperature, air movement, friction, and changes in amplitude can still affect accuracy considerably.

Many modern mechanical clocks use a balance wheel instead of a pendulum, especially watches. The balance wheel is a small wheel that rotates back and forth, attached to a spiral hairspring. Together, they form an oscillator. The hairspring pulls the balance wheel toward its central position, while inertia carries it past that position. The escapement gives the balance a periodic impulse and unlocks the gear train in response to each oscillation. Because a watch must function while moving, its oscillator is compact and often mounted with shock protection. Its rate is adjusted by changing spring tension or the regulator’s length.

The hands receive motion from the gear train through selected shafts. The center wheel usually turns once per hour and carries the minute hand, directly or through a motion works. A cannon pinion permits the hands to be set without disturbing the train. Gears reduce the minute-hand rotation to drive the hour hand. A seconds hand may be mounted on the fourth wheel, which turns once per minute. In a clock with a dial, the hands indicate time, although their movement may appear smooth only because the ticks occur many times each minute. In reality, the mechanism advances in increments.

Some mechanical clocks include a striking or chiming mechanism. A set of gears counts intervals and releases a hammer at the appropriate moment. The hammer hits a bell, gong, or tuned rod, producing the sound of an hour or quarter hour. This feature does not regulate time; it simply uses information from the timekeeping train. The case, plates, bridges, jewels, and lubricant support the moving parts. Jewels reduce wear at high-friction pivots, while the plates hold the wheels in precise alignment. Regular cleaning and oiling matter because dried lubricant and dust increase resistance, making the clock run slower or stop.
