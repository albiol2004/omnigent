A mechanical clock escapement is the mechanism that sits between the power source and the timekeeping oscillator, and it performs two inseparable jobs at once: it meters the release of stored energy into equal, countable steps, and it delivers a small kick to the oscillator on every cycle so the oscillator keeps moving against friction. Without it, a wound mainspring or a lifted weight would simply spin the gear train free in a second or two; with it, that same energy is doled out one tooth at a time, and each tooth becomes an audible tick. The name itself captures the idea — energy "escapes" in controlled increments rather than all at once. Every escapement is therefore a feedback loop: the oscillator gates the energy, and the energy sustains the oscillator.

The classic layout begins with the power source — a coiled mainspring in a watch, or a hanging weight in a longcase clock — which applies a steady torque to a train of gears. The train steps the speed down and the torque up through several wheels, ending at the escape wheel, a specially cut gear whose teeth are the actual "valve" of the system. Sitting across the escape wheel is a pair of pallets (often two jeweled stones on a fork-shaped lever) that alternately block and release the teeth. Beyond the pallets is the oscillator — a pendulum in clocks, or a balance wheel with a hairspring in watches — whose natural period is what the clock actually counts. A crutch or fork links the oscillator's swing to the pallet lever so that the oscillator's motion directly lifts and drops the pallets.

The cycle runs as follows. One pallet is locked against a tooth, holding the wheel still while the gear train strains against it. As the oscillator swings, it lifts that pallet just enough to let the tooth slip past; the wheel, driven by the train, advances by one tooth. The tooth's face and the pallet's impulse face are angled so that, as the tooth slides off, it pushes the pallet in the direction the oscillator is already moving — this is the impulse, the tiny energy transfer that re-energizes the swing. A heartbeat later the second pallet drops into the path of the next tooth and locks the wheel again, until the oscillator swings back the other way and the whole exchange repeats on the opposite side. One full oscillation thus yields two advances — the familiar tick and tock.

The geometry of those faces is where the real engineering lives, because the escapement must release and impulse the wheel while disturbing the oscillator's natural period as little as possible. The "lock" faces are cut with a slight draw so that, when a pallet settles against a tooth, the wheel's own torque nudges the pallet slightly deeper into engagement rather than letting it rattle free. The "drop" — the brief instant when neither pallet touches a tooth and the wheel runs unchecked — is kept as short as possible, because during the drop the oscillator is uncontrolled and the timing is at the mercy of the torque. The "lift" is the angular travel during which the impulse is delivered, and it must be large enough to sustain the swing yet small enough not to overdrive it. Designers trade these angles against each other to minimize what horologists call the escapement error: the small dependence of the rate on how hard the spring is wound.

Different escapements embody different compromises of this geometry. The verge and foliot, the earliest, lets the crown wheel recoil slightly backward at the end of each beat, which makes the hands visibly shudder but is forgiving to build. The dead-beat (Graham) escapement, perfected for pendulum clocks, is designed so the locking faces are concentric with the pallet axis: after the impulse the wheel simply stops dead rather than recoiling, which removes a source of error and lets the second hand land cleanly on each mark. The lever escapement, dominant in watches, isolates the balance wheel from the gear train except during the brief impulse and unlock, so the oscillator is nearly free for most of its arc — a property called "detachment" that is the key to good watch timekeeping. Each is a different answer to the same question: how do you let energy out and push the oscillator while touching it as little as possible?

Ultimately the clock's accuracy rests on the oscillator, not the escapement: a pendulum's period depends almost entirely on its length and on gravity (and is nearly independent of amplitude at small angles, the property called isochronism), while a balance wheel's period depends on its moment of inertia and the hairspring's stiffness. The escapement's task is to be a faithful servant — to count the oscillator's beats by releasing one tooth per beat, to feed back just enough energy to replace what friction takes, and to add as little of its own timing disturbance as the craft allows. When all of this is in balance, the stored energy of a spring or a weight is transmuted, tick by tick, into the steady, audible measurement of time.
