A mechanical clock escapement is the heart of any mechanical timekeeper — the mechanism that converts the steady, unregulated pull of a weight or a wound mainspring into the discrete, evenly spaced ticks we hear as the clock runs. Without an escapement, a clock's gear train would simply spin freely under the power source until the energy was exhausted in a few seconds. The escapement sits at the junction between the power train and the timekeeping element, and it performs two simultaneous duties: it releases the stored energy in tiny, equal increments governed by the oscillator, and it delivers a small impulse to that oscillator on each cycle to replace the energy lost to friction and air resistance. In this way the escapement is both a governor and a sustainer, and the precision of a clock depends almost entirely on how well its escapement does these two jobs.

The power source — typically a slowly descending weight in a tall case clock or a coiled mainspring in a smaller clock or watch — applies a constant torque to the first wheel of the gear train. That torque is stepped down through a series of gears and pinions, each successive wheel turning faster but with less force, until it reaches the escape wheel, which is the final and fastest-turning gear in the train. The escape wheel is the last gear that is driven by the power source; beyond it, the motion is controlled rather than driven. The shape and spacing of the escape wheel's teeth are carefully designed so that only one tooth can pass the pallets at a time, and so that each passing tooth can both be arrested and deliver a measured push.

The escape wheel itself is a specialized gear whose teeth are not symmetrical like ordinary gear teeth but are shaped — often as angled or raked sawteeth — to interact with two pallets that alternately catch and release them. In a typical pendulum clock with an anchor or deadbeat escapement, the escape wheel has thirty teeth and advances exactly one tooth per beat, so that one full revolution of the escape wheel equals sixty beats or thirty seconds at a one-second beat. The geometry of each tooth's lifting face and locking face is critical: the lifting face must give the pallet a push at the right angle to transfer energy efficiently, while the locking face must bring the wheel to a clean stop without bouncing or recoiling more than intended.

Opposing the escape wheel is the oscillator, which provides the actual timekeeping reference. In larger clocks this is a pendulum, whose period depends almost entirely on its length and is remarkably insensitive to the amplitude of swing or to small changes in drive force; in watches and smaller clocks it is a balance wheel paired with a hairspring, whose period depends on the wheel's moment of inertia and the spring's stiffness. The oscillator swings back and forth at a natural rate determined by its physical properties, and it is this rate — not the speed of the gear train — that sets how often the escapement allows a tooth to escape. Because the oscillator's period is stable, the clock keeps time; because the escapement feeds it a little energy each beat, the oscillator never runs down.

Connecting the two is the pallet assembly — in a pendulum clock, an anchor-shaped piece that rocks with the pendulum's swing and presents two pallets (an entry pallet and an exit pallet) to the escape wheel teeth. The cycle of a single beat runs as follows: one pallet slides into the path of an approaching tooth and catches it on its locking face, halting the wheel with a click; as the oscillator continues its swing and reverses direction, that pallet withdraws and releases the tooth, allowing the wheel to advance; the tooth's lifting face rides across the pallet face and pushes the pallet forward, giving the oscillator its sustaining impulse; and finally the opposite pallet swings into position to catch the next tooth, producing the second click. The two clicks, one for each pallet, are the familiar tick and tock.

Thus the complete action of the escapement is a self-sustaining loop: the oscillator governs the release of power, and the released power repays the oscillator with the energy it needs to keep governing. This interplay is what allows a mechanical clock to run for days on a single winding while dividing time into equal portions. Different escapement designs — the ancient verge and foliot, the anchor with its slight recoil, the deadbeat that eliminates recoil for better regulation, the lever escapement that isolates the balance wheel in watches, and the tourbillon that rotates the whole escapement to average out positional errors — all solve the same two problems with different geometries, trading simplicity, robustness, and accuracy against one another. In every case the escapement remains the single component where the physics of energy transfer and the art of horology meet most intimately, and its refinement over centuries is largely the story of how clocks became accurate.
